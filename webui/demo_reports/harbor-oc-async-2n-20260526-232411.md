## 1. Executive Summary

This 2-node fully-async GSPO run on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct shows encouraging reward improvement (`critic/score/mean` rising from 0.316 to 0.432, a 36% gain over 17 steps), but suffers from a critical policy optimization pathology: both `actor/pg_clipfrac` and `actor/ppo_kl` are identically zero across all 17 steps, meaning the clipped surrogate objective is not actively shaping the policy in any detectable way. The training reward gains are real but appear driven almost entirely by distribution shift in sampling (longer responses, more turns) rather than by meaningful gradient signal from the GSPO loss. Validation scores are flat at ~0.17, well below training scores of ~0.43, confirming that the policy has not meaningfully generalized. Infrastructure efficiency is improving (trainer idle ratio dropped from 0.474 to 0.103, throughput nearly doubled), but tail latency in Harbor sandbox execution is growing and will become a bottleneck. The single highest-priority action is diagnosing and resolving the zero-KL / zero-clipfrac issue, without which the run is consuming GPU-hours for negligible policy learning.

## 2. Reward & Score Signal

### Training Reward

The `critic/score/mean` trajectory from 0.316 to 0.432 looks superficially healthy. With a binary 0/1 reward from the verifier, this means the solve rate rose from ~31.6% to ~43.2% over 17 steps. The `critic/advantages/mean` shifting from -0.031 to +0.144 is consistent with a population that is, on average, doing slightly better than baseline expectation.

However, the **advantage saturation** is a red flag: `critic/advantages/max` and `critic/advantages/min` are locked at ±2.475 across all steps. This is almost certainly a hard clamp applied in the advantage normalization or value function. With a binary reward and `rollout_n=8`, the advantage distribution is inherently bimodal (you either solved it or you didn't), and clamping at ±2.475 means the tails are being truncated. This is not necessarily harmful for binary rewards — the information content is limited anyway — but it does mean the gradient signal from high-advantage outliers is artificially capped. Verify whether this clamp is a verl default or a configuration choice; for binary rewards, a wider or absent clamp may yield marginally stronger signal on the rare tasks that only one of eight rollouts solves.

### Validation Gap

Validation scores tell the real story: 0.162 → 0.174 → 0.170 across steps 5, 10, and 15. This is essentially flat and sits at roughly **40% of the training score** at corresponding steps. Two explanations are plausible:

1. **Selection bias in training batches**: the async queue may be non-uniformly sampling easier tasks (shorter Harbor execution times complete first and dominate the queue), inflating training score relative to a held-out uniform evaluation.
2. **No real policy improvement**: if the policy is not updating meaningfully (see Section 3), the training score increase may reflect the model getting "luckier" with longer responses and more turns on the same task distribution, while validation on a fixed set shows no gain.

The gap is too large to ignore. A training score of 0.43 with a validation score of 0.17 is not a sign of overfitting in the classical sense (only 17 steps) — it is a sign that the training metric is misleading.

## 3. Policy Optimization Dynamics

### The Zero-KL / Zero-Clipfrac Problem

This is the most critical finding in the report. Across all 17 steps:

- `actor/pg_clipfrac` = 0.0
- `actor/ppo_kl` = 0.0

In a functioning PPO/GSPO loop, `pg_clipfrac` should be nonzero (typically 0.05–0.20 for well-tuned runs) and `ppo_kl` should be small but positive (1e-4 to 1e-2). Both being exactly zero means one of the following:

1. **The log-ratio (log π_θ / π_old) is exactly zero for every token in every sequence.** This would happen if the "old" policy and the "current" policy are literally the same parameters — i.e., the actor weights are not being updated, or the reference log-probs are being recomputed from the current policy rather than the rollout policy.
2. **A numerical or implementation bug** is zeroing out the ratio before the clip operation. For instance, if `old_log_probs` is inadvertently being set equal to `new_log_probs` at each step (a common async-PPO bug where the rollout policy reference is refreshed from the current actor instead of being stored at rollout time).
3. **The staleness mechanism is discarding all samples.** With `staleness_threshold=1`, any sample generated more than 1 step ago is rejected. If the async pipeline is slow enough that most samples arrive stale, the effective batch might be empty or near-empty, and the loss is computed over a degenerate set.

The fact that `actor/pg_loss` is nonzero and increasing in magnitude (-0.00183 → -0.00691) seems to contradict hypothesis (1), but GSPO's loss formulation includes terms beyond the standard clipped ratio (the group scoring component), which could produce a nonzero loss even when the ratio is 1.0 everywhere. The gradient norms (`actor/grad_norm` in the range 0.44–1.88) confirm that *some* gradient is flowing, but if the ratio is always 1.0, these gradients are not coming from the PPO surrogate — they may be from auxiliary terms, entropy bonus, or numerical noise.

**This must be investigated immediately.** Instrument the training loop to log the raw log-ratio distribution (mean, std, max, min) at each step. Check whether `old_log_probs` in the replay buffer matches the actor's `new_log_probs` at the time of the update.

### Entropy

`actor/entropy` rising from 0.172 to 0.199 is a modestly positive sign — the policy is not collapsing. However, this is also consistent with the policy simply not changing: if no meaningful update is applied, entropy will drift slightly with the sampling distribution but not collapse.

### Policy Loss Trajectory

The `actor/pg_loss` moving from -0.00183 to -0.00691 indicates the loss magnitude is growing. In GSPO, a more negative loss means the optimizer is finding larger "gains" to exploit — but with zero clip fraction, the clipping constraint is never active, which means either (a) the gains are tiny and never hit the clip threshold, or (b) the ratio is always 1.0 and the loss structure is an artifact.

## 4. Data & Sampling

### Response Length Growth

`response_length/mean` growing from 9,549 to 12,426 tokens (+30%) and `num_turns/mean` from 7.5 to 11.4 is a significant distributional shift. The model is producing longer, more turn-heavy trajectories. This could be:

- **Positive**: the model is learning to persist longer on harder tasks, trying more approaches.
- **Neutral/negative**: the model is generating verbose, repetitive outputs without improving solve rate (consistent with the flat validation score).

The `response_length/max` hitting 68,000 (the `max_new_tokens` limit) and `response_length/clip_ratio` rising to 0.012 means about 1.2% of rollouts are being truncated. This is not yet alarming but is trending in the wrong direction.

### Turn Count Outliers

`num_turns/max` exploding from 64 to 880 is pathological. An 880-turn agent interaction is almost certainly a degenerate loop (the agent repeatedly attempting the same failed action). These outliers do not necessarily dominate training (they are a single worst-case in the batch), but they inflate `harbor_agent_execute/max` and contribute to tail latency. Consider implementing a hard turn cap (e.g., 100 turns) at the agent level, independent of the token-level `max_new_tokens` limit.

### Async Queue Dynamics

`max_concurrent_samples=64` with `max_queue_size=128` and `required_samples=64` means the trainer waits for 64 completed samples before each update. With `staleness_threshold=1`, samples from step N-2 or earlier are discarded. This is aggressive — if a training step takes long enough that some in-flight rollouts become stale, they are wasted. The `fully_async/trainer/idle_ratio` dropping from 0.474 to 0.103 suggests the pipeline is now well-fed, but the growing `fully_async/processing_time/tp99` (447s → 846s) means the tail of rollouts is taking longer, which will eventually cause the trainer to idle again as it waits for the last few samples to fill the batch.

## 6. Training Stability & Off-Policy Correction

### Rollout Correlation Metrics

- `rollout_corr/kl` decreasing from 0.190 to 0.147: this measures the KL between the rollout policy and the current policy at update time. The decrease is consistent with the policy barely changing (confirming the zero-KL finding from Section 3).
- `rollout_corr/training_ppl` at 1.40–1.43 with one spike to 5.68: the spike deserves investigation (likely a batch with unusually long or degenerate sequences), but the baseline is stable.
- `rollout_corr/chi2_seq` near -1.0: this indicates the sequence-level chi-squared divergence is tightly bounded, consistent with near-on-policy samples (because the policy is barely moving).
- `training/rollout_probs_diff_mean` at 0.052–0.062: the mean absolute difference between rollout and training log-probs is small, again consistent with minimal policy change.

All stability metrics paint the same picture: the policy is barely changing between steps. The off-policy correction is functioning correctly (stale samples are rejected, rollout-training divergence is small), but this is trivially achieved when the policy itself is near-stationary.

## 7. Model & Architecture Considerations

Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct is a Mixture-of-Experts model with 30B total parameters but only ~3B active per token. This has specific implications:

1. **Expert routing instability under RL**: MoE models can exhibit expert collapse or routing oscillation when the loss landscape changes abruptly. The stable `actor/grad_norm` (0.44–1.88) suggests this is not occurring, but the zero-KL issue may be masking it.
2. **FSDP + MoE interaction**: with `fsdp_size=8`, each FSDP rank holds a shard of all experts. The Ulysses SP-4 adds sequence parallelism. This is a correct configuration for memory efficiency, but the combined effect on gradient synchronization with sparse expert activation should be verified — specifically, ensure that expert gradients are correctly aggregated across FSDP ranks even when an expert is not activated on some ranks.
3. **vLLM TP=4 for inference**: with `gpu_memory_utilization=0.85`, this should comfortably serve the model. The `max_new_tokens=68000` is very large; combined with `temperature=1.0`, this allows highly diverse and long rollouts, which is appropriate for exploration but contributes to the response length growth.

## 8. Top-Priority Recommendations

### 1. Diagnose and Fix the Zero-KL / Zero-Clipfrac Pathology
**Difficulty**: Medium | **Impact**: Critical

The PPO/GSPO surrogate objective is not functioning. Without a nonzero importance ratio, the clipped policy gradient reduces to a constant and provides no learning signal from the reward.

**Action**: Add logging for the per-token log-ratio `log(π_θ(a|s) / π_old(a|s))` — mean, std, min, max — at each training step. Check that `old_log_probs` stored in the replay buffer at rollout time are genuinely from the rollout policy and not being overwritten. In the async pipeline, verify that the policy checkpoint used for rollout generation is properly snapshotted and not a reference to the live actor. Examine the verl GSPO implementation for any code path that normalizes or zeros the ratio.

### 2. Close the Training-Validation Gap
**Difficulty**: Easy | **Impact**: High

A 0.43 training score vs. 0.17 validation score (2.5× gap) means the training metric is not predictive of generalization.

**Action**: Log per-task solve rates to check whether the async queue is biased toward easier (faster-completing) tasks. Run a synchronous validation sweep on the same task distribution used for training to isolate the bias. If the gap persists, the training score itself should be discounted in decision-making.

### 3. Cap Agent Turn Count and Investigate Degenerate Loops
**Difficulty**: Easy | **Impact**: Medium

880 turns is pathological and wastes sandbox time and queue slots.

**Action**: Implement a hard turn cap at 100–150 turns at the agent level. Log and inspect the 880-turn trajectory to identify the degenerate loop pattern — this may indicate a prompt or tool-use failure mode that can be patched in the agent system prompt. Additionally, consider tightening `agent_max_timeout` from 2,400s to 1,800s, or enabling the tail-killer (`tail_kill_target=0.95`, `tail_kill_grace_sec=180`) to reclaim resources from stalled rollouts.

### 4. Increase Learning Rate or PPO Epochs
**Difficulty**: Easy | **Impact**: High (after fix #1)

Even after the zero-KL bug is fixed, `lr=1e-6` with `ppo_epochs=1` is extremely conservative for a 3B-active-parameter model. The current gradient norms (0.44–1.88) suggest headroom for a larger step.

**Action**: Once the ratio is confirmed nonzero, increase the learning rate to 5e-6 or 1e-5 with a short warmup (5–10 steps). Alternatively, increase `ppo_epochs` to 2–4 to extract more signal per batch. Monitor `actor/ppo_kl` to ensure it stays below 0.02 and `pg_clipfrac` stays below 0.25.

### 5. Investigate Harbor Verifier Spike and Harden Tail Latency
**Difficulty**: Medium | **Impact**: Medium

The 6,002s verifier spike (100 minutes for what should be a <60s operation) indicates an infrastructure failure, likely K8s pod scheduling delay or resource contention.

**Action**: Add a hard timeout on the verifier (120s) with automatic retry. For the broader tail latency trend (`harbor_total/max` 717s → 1,320s), enable the tail-killer to prevent the slowest 5% of rollouts from blocking batch completion. Monitor K8s node utilization to ensure sandbox pods are not being preempted or throttled.
